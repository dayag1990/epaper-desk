The last ferryman of the Wren

For forty-one years Walter Pike has rowed people across a river that now has two bridges. He is not ready to stop.

By Hannah Oakes · Riverton, 17 Nov · 2026-11-17

At twenty past seven on a grey Tuesday morning, Walter Pike unties a green rowing boat from the landing stage below Tanners Quay and waits. Nobody is there yet. He says that is normal. People arrive late, and then all at once.

Pike, 68, is the last working ferryman on the Wren. For forty-one years he has carried passengers between the quay and the meadows on the far bank, a crossing of about ninety metres. Two bridges now stand within a ten-minute walk, one of them recently saved by the council, and nobody needs the ferry to get across. They take it anyway.

A family trade

The ferry licence was first granted to a Pike in 1874, by the old borough. Walter's great-grandfather rowed it, then his grandfather, then an uncle. His father worked at the sawmill and wanted something different for his son, and Walter spent his twenties as a van driver before the uncle fell ill and asked him to take a turn.

He says he meant to stay a month. He has the original receipt for the first fare, a shilling, folded in a tobacco tin in the hut by the water. The fare is now two pounds, or free for children and anyone carrying a dog he likes.

The river tells you things. You learn what the water will do before it does it. You cannot learn that from a book.

The regulars

A woman in a yellow coat arrives at half past seven, holding a thermos. She is Eileen Foss, a retired nurse, and she has crossed with Pike every working morning since 2011 to walk her terrier on the meadows. A pair of students from Riverton College come next, laughing, one of them clutching a bicycle wheel. A man in a suit asks to be dropped at the far bank so that he can have a quiet phone call, and Pike obliges without comment.

Few of them have any practical need for the boat. Foss says she likes that it is slow. The students say it is the best part of their commute. The man in the suit says it is the only place in the town where nobody expects him to answer his emails.

Pike keeps a log of every crossing in school exercise books. There are 112 of them, stacked in a cupboard. The total of passengers, by his reckoning, is somewhere near three hundred and ten thousand.

Not every crossing is gentle. In 2019 the flood took the landing stage and left the boat in a farmer's field two miles downstream. Pike fetched it with a borrowed tractor and had it back in service within the week. He says he was more annoyed about the logbook, which got wet.

There was the night, in the winter of 1996, when he rowed a midwife across in thick fog because the bridge had been closed by ice. The baby was born healthy on the meadow side an hour later. The boy, now a man of thirty, buys Pike a pint every birthday.

Rules, rust and a retirement

Running the ferry is not easy money. The licence demands an annual safety inspection, insurance that costs more each year and a lifejacket for every passenger. The boat itself, built in 1987 by a yard that no longer exists, needs new planking every few winters. Pike pays for most of it himself, along with the small grant the council gives for the heritage service.

Last spring, after a bad bout of pneumonia, he told his family he would stop at the end of the season. His daughter, Jess, a joiner in Marlow, drove up and spent a weekend mending the hut roof. She did not say anything about retirement. On Monday she asked how one went about getting a ferry licence.

Jess Pike, 37, now crosses with her father two days a week and has passed the boat-handling course. She says she is not sure whether she will do it for life. She is sure she does not want it to end because nobody was there to pick up the oars.

What the town owes

Some in Riverton have started to say the town owes the ferry something. A group called Friends of the Ferry has collected nearly £7,000 for a new boat. The council's heritage officer, Daniel Rowe, says that a place on the town's official list of cultural assets would give the crossing some protection, and would help with insurance costs.

Pike is a little embarrassed by the fuss. He says the boat is not a monument, just a way across. Then he looks at the water, which is brown and fast after a week of rain, and says that nobody ought to be asked to love a monument, but they might love a ferry.

At ten past eight the last of the morning passengers steps off at the meadows. Pike turns the boat with one stroke, a movement so small it is hard to see, and heads back across the river. The quay is full of people waiting.

He estimates there will be about thirty crossings today. He has not yet decided what to do about the new boat. He says the oars, at least, will be the same.
